Prioritizing Content for Human Review Using Search and Engagement Signals

A comparison of an interpretable CTR-based baseline, Logistic Regression, and Random Forest on a client-grouped holdout.

Author: Melike Kaya Primary metric: Precision@50 Test records: 6,163
Abstract Problem Data Methodology Results Limitations Recommendations Reproducibility Credit

Abstract

This study asks whether historical search visibility, click-through, engagement, and content-update signals can be used to prioritize content records for human review. Using a public-safe anonymized dataset from the FlyRank ML Internship, I compared an interpretable fixed-rule baseline with Logistic Regression and Random Forest on a client-grouped holdout split. The methods were evaluated primarily with Precision@50 because the operational objective is to place useful candidates near the top of a limited review queue. The fixed-rule baseline achieved the strongest Precision@50, while Random Forest achieved the highest ROC-AUC, showing that broader discrimination and top-of-queue usefulness can lead to different model choices. The results support using an interpretable ranked queue as a human decision aid, but they do not prove that acting on the recommendations will improve business performance.

Introduction / Problem Statement

Content teams may have thousands of pages to inspect but limited time for manual review. The practical decision is therefore which records should be examined first.

This project evaluates whether historical search and engagement signals can create a useful ranked queue for editors and SEO reviewers. The system is a prioritization tool and does not automatically edit, delete, or publish content.

Research question: Can public-safe historical signals rank content records that are more likely to require human review?

Data

The project uses the public-safe, anonymized dataset provided through the FlyRank ML Internship. The release was derived from approximately 79 million rows of production search and analytics data.

Modeling and ranking used the prepared content_refresh_anonymized.csv table containing 30,000 content-level records and 44 columns. Signals include impressions, clicks, click-through rate, average search position, engagement measures, content freshness, and recent performance information.

March 2026 was used as the feature window in the forward-looking data contract, while April 2026 was used as the outcome window.

What was excluded and why

Client and content identifiers were excluded from model features because they identify or group records rather than represent transferable behavior. Fields directly used to construct the target, including trend_direction and trend_pct, were excluded to reduce leakage. No client names, page URLs, private search queries, credentials, or other identifying information appear in this public page.

Methodology

Label and assumptions

The target is a binary decline indicator based on future-period performance. Historical visibility, engagement, freshness, and behavior signals were treated as ranking inputs, not as proof that an edit would cause improvement.

Baseline and models

The Week 4 CTR baseline ranks records using an interpretable action score based on visible search demand, search position, weak click-through performance, and related opportunity signals. It was compared with Logistic Regression and Random Forest.

Validation design

The data was split by client rather than by random row. Training contained 23,837 records from 25 clients, while testing contained 6,163 records from 7 unseen clients. There was no client overlap between the two groups.

Evaluation

Precision@50 was selected as the primary operational metric because the review process focuses on a small queue. Precision@20, Recall@50, Average Precision, ROC-AUC, and the test-set base rate were also reported.

The baseline Precision@50 of 0.68 means that 34 of the first 50 recommendations were positive cases on the held-out test set. Logistic Regression achieved 0.64 and Random Forest achieved 0.58.

Decision: retain the interpretable CTR baseline as the initial queue generator for this dataset, split, label definition, and review capacity.

Limitations & Honest Framing

This study is an offline evaluation. It does not measure what happens after reviewers act and does not prove that editing a recommended page will improve traffic, engagement, or business outcomes.

The label is a proxy for future decline, not a direct measure of whether a page should be refreshed. Seasonality, competition, search demand, SERP changes, technical problems, and other unobserved factors may affect the outcome.

Baseline thresholds and weights were manually designed, the machine learning models were not exhaustively tuned, and the conclusions come from one prepared dataset and one grouped holdout split.

Ranked Recommendations

1. Review title and meta information

Prioritize pages with meaningful visibility but weak click-through. Compare titles and descriptions with likely search intent and the promise made by the page.

2. Refresh visible but aging content

Check whether dates, facts, examples, screenshots, and key sections remain current. Update only after manual inspection.

3. Investigate engagement weakness

Review content-message alignment, readability, internal links, calls to action, page speed, and layout issues.

4. Reassess low-confidence candidates

Keep low-volume and borderline records lower in the queue, gather more observations, and consider seasonality before acting.

Review the top 50 records manually, document the action taken, and collect post-review outcomes for future threshold recalibration and model improvement.
